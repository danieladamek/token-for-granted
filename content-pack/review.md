# Token for Granted: routes to grant money for universities and non-profits

<!-- section: abstract -->
## Abstract

A grant is not one thing. Federal law separates a grant from a cooperative agreement and from a contract, and separates money an agency awards by competition from money a statute sends to the states by formula [1851] [1880]. Foundations are different again, and so is a project that Congress names in an appropriations act [2237] [1667]. This guide maps the routes to that money for a university researcher, a research office or a non-profit that has not applied before. It covers research project grants, fellowships and training awards, programme and service grants, and capacity and infrastructure money, from federal agencies and from 65 foundations and other private grantmakers, a sample chosen by the editor [2124] [2290].

Each route carries the gates in front of it, from registration in SAM.gov, which the registry says can take up to 10 business days and one agency advises starting 90 days ahead, to tax-exempt recognition for a new non-profit [1897] [161] [2053]. Each is read against the rules beneath every federal award, the Uniform Guidance in its 2024 text [1799] [1883]. A dated account records what moved in 2025 and 2026: four agency caps on indirect cost rates that courts set aside, grant terminations and the suits over them, competitions cancelled or moved between departments, and a proposed rewrite of the grant rules that cannot be made final before December 11, 2026 [24] [544] [1594] [1480]. The guide describes and does not advise. It is not peer-reviewed. It was closed on October 5, 2026.

## Why this matters: one word, several systems

A principal investigator, a research office and the director of a shelter can all say that they need a grant, and between them mean four different things. One is a competition run by a federal agency [1851]. Another is money that a statute sends to the states by formula and that never passes through a federal application [1880]. A third is a foundation, which may or may not read what it is sent [2259]. The fourth, which few newcomers know exists, is a line in an appropriations act with a recipient's name on it [1667]. Which of these a reader is facing decides who applies, to whom, by when and under which rules.

<!-- figure: four-doors -->

The competition is the door most people picture, and even it is not quite what they picture. The applicant is an organisation: at NSF an organisation submits the proposal, and an unaffiliated individual is not eligible for direct funding [156] [158]. Reviewers advise and the agency decides [157] [38]. Since August 2025 a senior appointee also reviews funding announcements and discretionary awards before they issue [246]. The figures a newcomer has heard may be out of date as well, since NIH reported that its success rate for research project grants fell from 18.5% in FY 2024 to 13.0% in FY 2025 [81].

Formula money has no federal door at all. Older Americans Act money passes from the federal agency to 56 state agencies on aging, then to 618 area agencies, then to nearly 20,000 local service providers [1260]. The SAMHSA block grants, the Title V block grant and 21st Century Community Learning Centers go to states in the same way [1130] [1168] [1399]. FEMA's notice for its Nonprofit Security Grant Program says in so many words that non-profit organisations may not apply to FEMA directly [1320]. A non-profit looking at one of them has no federal application to write; what it writes goes to the state or local body that holds the money [1880] [1823].

Foundations are the third system, and the largest of them are the least like a competition. Of the 65 foundations and private grantmakers with a record in this guide, 23 fund by invitation only and 23 only through calls they post themselves, while six accept an unsolicited request [2259] [2119] [2310]. Many of their fellowships cannot be applied for directly: the researcher's own institution nominates, under a cap the foundation sets [2174]. The sums are not small. Giving USA puts giving by foundations at $117.15 billion in 2025 [2409].

Then there is Congress. It may name a recipient, a project and an amount in an appropriations act [1667] [1674]. No organisation applies to a committee for that. It asks its own House Member or a Senator, whose office decides whether to submit the request, and a recipient named in the act still applies to the agency before any money moves [1667] [1686].

Whatever the door, something stands in front of it. A first federal application needs registrations that the registry itself says can take up to 10 business days, and for which NIH tells applicants to allow six weeks and NSF 90 days [1897] [1909] [161]. Eligibility may turn on the institution's state: NSF lists 28 jurisdictions for its EPSCoR programmes and NIH 23 states and Puerto Rico for IDeA, and the two lists are not the same places [1517] [1543]. For a new non-profit the first gate is recognition of its tax exemption [2046].

All of this would be worth a map in a quiet year, and 2025 and 2026 were not quiet. Four agencies announced a 15 percent cap on universities' indirect cost rates, and a court set each one aside [24] [1852] [237] [1855] [1854]. HHS issued notices on March 24 and 25, 2025 terminating about $11 billion in public health grants, and the injunction that followed covers the states that sued [544]. The Education Department stopped awarding discretionary grants under seven programmes for designated institutions and placed other competitions with the Department of Labor [1594] [672]. OMB proposed to rewrite the rules beneath every federal award, and Congress barred the rewrite from being made final before December 11, 2026 [1458] [1480]. A page an agency wrote in early 2025 may therefore describe a policy that no longer exists, or one that holds for some recipients and not for others [24] [544]. <!-- synthesis -->

Taken together, these facts set the shape of what follows: routes first, each with the gates in front of it, read against the rules underneath, and beside them a dated record of what changed, so that a reader can tell a rule in force from one that was vacated and from one that is only proposed. <!-- synthesis -->
The sections move from how the money is put together to who gives it: how an award works, the rules beneath it, the research funders, awards that follow a person, programme and service money, foundations, standing and capacity, the gates, and then the dated account of 2025 and 2026.

## How grant money moves

A newcomer tends to picture a grant as a sum of money handed to a person with a good idea, chosen by a panel of experts. The money moves under a legal instrument that statute selects, it goes to an organisation, it arrives on a calendar the funder sets, and the experts who read the application do not decide [1851] [156] [157].

Federal law gives an agency three instruments for giving money to an outside body, and the choice follows from the purpose of the transaction [1851]. A procurement contract buys property or services for the Government's own direct benefit or use [1851]. A grant and a cooperative agreement both transfer something of value to carry out a public purpose that a law authorises, and what separates the two is whether substantial involvement is expected between agency and recipient while the work is carried out [1851]. The statute does not define that involvement, and a notice for a cooperative agreement has to describe what the agency expects [1848]. At NIH such awards carry U codes, and each notice names the NIH roles and a dispute panel [106] [116].

A second division cuts across the first: how the recipient and the amount are chosen [1880]. For a discretionary grant the agency may select the recipient from among all eligible applicants and decides the amount [1880]. A mandatory grant leaves no such room: the authorising statute requires an award to each eligible entity in the amount or by the formula it sets, and a formula grant is an allocation to states, territories or local governments [1880]. Each programme's entry in the Assistance Listings, the public catalogue of federal assistance programmes, states which kind its awards are [1808] [1799].

The distinction decides whether an organisation competes before a federal agency or applies to a state or local recipient [1880] [1805]. Older Americans Act money takes the second path, from ACL to 56 state agencies on aging, then 618 area agencies on aging, then nearly 20,000 local service providers [1260]. The non-profit at the end is a subrecipient of the state [1823] [1880]. The notice rules in 2 CFR 200.204 apply to discretionary funding opportunities, and Executive Order 14332 applies to discretionary grants only [1800] [246]. What follows about notices and reviewers is therefore an account of discretionary money; the formula lines, taken up in a later section, run through a state. <!-- synthesis -->

On the discretionary side the applicant is seldom a person: NSF's ordinary route has an organisation submit a full proposal, and unaffiliated individuals are not eligible for direct funding [156] [157] [158]. At NIH the organisation registers in SAM, Grants.gov and eRA Commons, while each principal investigator links an ORCID iD to the eRA Commons account [4] [31]. Before an award the agency reviews the risk the applicant poses, and that assessment may weigh financial stability, management systems and audit findings [1809]. Those are an institution's properties, which fits a system where the investigator is named and the organisation applies [1809] [4] [156]. <!-- synthesis --> NEH's Division of Research, an exception, listed four programmes for individuals when its page was read [635].

Discretionary money is announced in a notice of funding opportunity, the formal announcement that federal funding is available through a financial assistance programme [1799]. The Uniform Guidance fixes what a notice must contain and in what order, and a summary of each is displayed on Grants.gov [1800] [1848]. From fiscal year 2026 Grants.gov is NIH's single official source, whereas NSF also posts in its own Funding Search and on directorate pages [9] [10] [155]. A rule OMB proposed on May 29, 2026, not yet final, would require every federal funding opportunity to be posted on Grants.gov, with a national security exception [1803] [1458].

The same guidance sets a standard of at least 60 calendar days open for application, with a floor of 30 absent exigent circumstances, though worded as an expectation, not a requirement [1800]. Inside that frame each funder keeps its own rhythm: NIH runs three review cycles a year [2]. At NSF each opportunity sets its own timing, which may be a deadline date, a submission window or acceptance at any time [156]. SAMHSA's FY 2026 service notices were posted between June 15 and July 1, 2026, and most closed in about a month [1109] [1208] [1207]. A programme agency's window can sit well inside the sixty-day standard [1800] [1109]. <!-- synthesis -->

Review comes next. At NIH it has two levels: a scientific review group, called a study section, and after it the national advisory council or board of the institute that would pay [38]. Reviewers of most research project applications score two factors from 1 to 9 [39] [41]. From the January 2026 council round institutes do not rely on paylines, and each publishes the factors it weighs [123] [15].

At NSF a programme officer obtains reviews from ad hoc reviewers, a panel or both, then makes a recommendation to award or decline [157]. Since December 8, 2025 at least two reviewers are required, and they may be NSF staff [149]. The policy guide as printed places final programmatic approval at division or office level; after a December 2025 realignment, the pages read do not name the post that now gives it [157] [168]. SAMHSA has peer reviewers score the project narrative against point-weighted sections; the programme office and an approving official make the final determination, and awards over $250,000 need National Advisory Council approval [549] [1107] [1113] [1112] [548]. The procedures differ, yet in each the reviewers' scores go to the agency and the agency makes the choice [123] [157] [549]. <!-- synthesis -->

<!-- figure: who-decides -->

A further layer dates from 2025: since Executive Order 14332 a senior appointee in each agency reviews new funding opportunity announcements and discretionary grants before they issue [246]. The review sits on top of merit or peer review and checks consistency with agency priorities and the national interest, and appointees are not to ministerially ratify or routinely defer to what others propose [246]. That is the rule in force [246]. OMB's proposed rule of May 29, 2026 would write a pre-issuance review into 2 CFR 200.205: senior appointees review proposals selected for funding, the review may form the basis of a decision not to select an applicant, and peer review remains advisory [2092] [2093] [2104].

What a successful applicant receives is a document, and every funding notice has a section on award notices [1848]. At NIH the Notice of Award is the official award document; the recipient accepts by drawing down or requesting funds and is then bound by the NIH Grants Policy Statement unless the notice says otherwise [122] [118]. The award can arrive with specific conditions attached [1886]. Nor does it settle every later year, since SAMHSA releases each later year's funds on approval of progress reports [549] [1107] [1113] [1112] [548].

## The rules underneath

A researcher holding one award from NIH and another from NSF might expect two sets of rules for spending the money, one per agency. Underneath both sits the same text: the Uniform Guidance, the government-wide set of administrative requirements, cost principles and audit requirements for federal awards, issued by the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) as 2 CFR part 200 [1799] [1805]. It binds the agencies that make awards and, through the award terms, recipients and subrecipients [1805]. The text in force on October 4, 2026 is the revision published April 22, 2024 at 89 FR 30046, effective October 1, 2024 [1883] [1801].

On May 29, 2026 OMB and the grant-making agencies published a proposed rule to revise 2 CFR, part 200 included [1458]. It would turn the guidance into a regulation that takes effect government-wide on one date and change rules on payments, subawards, termination and cost items, among others [1803] [1458]. Comments were due July 13, 2026, and the Federal Register record shows 496,606 of them [1458] [1858].

OMB proposed that a final rule take effect by October 1, 2026 [2093] [2104]. No final rule had been published by October 4, 2026, and OMB's regulatory agenda of August 14, 2026 lists it in the proposed rule stage with no date for final action [1858] [1859]. Section 157 of the continuing resolution signed September 2, 2026 then barred a rule "arising out of" the proposal "or a substantially similar rule" from being issued or finalised through December 11, 2026 [1480]. The date OMB proposed has passed with the 2024 text still the rule in force, and until a final rule exists the proposal applies to no one [2093] [1857] [1480]. <!-- synthesis -->

Indirect costs are costs incurred for a common or joint purpose that cannot readily be assigned to the award that benefits [1799]. At major universities and major non-profits they fall into two groups, Facilities and Administration, hence the other name, F&A costs [1802]. An award pays them as a percentage, the indirect cost rate, applied to a base of direct costs [1849] [1850]. An organisation gets its rate in one of two ways: a negotiated agreement with its cognizant federal agency, or the de minimis rate [1802].

A negotiated rate is settled by one agency on behalf of all federal agencies, and under 2 CFR 200.414(c)(1) all of them must accept it [1799] [1802]. An agency may use a different rate only when a federal statute or regulation requires it, or when the awarding agency approves a deviation under procedures it has made public [1802]. HHS regulations, for instance, limit indirect costs on training grants to 8% of modified total direct costs [1884].

The de minimis rate serves an organisation with no current negotiated rate, a position many small non-profits and first-time recipients are in [1802]. Under 200.414(f) such an organisation may elect a rate of up to 15 percent of modified total direct costs and use it indefinitely, with no documentation needed [1802]. Agency pages do not agree on the figure: HHS states 15% of modified total direct costs, while the NIH policy text as fetched gives 10% [528] [64] [36].

That 15 percent is in force [1802]. The other 15 percent belongs to 2025, when four agencies announced that they would pay universities indirect costs at that figure in place of negotiated rates: NIH on February 7, DOE on April 11, NSF on May 2 and the Department of Defense on May 14 [24] [1852] [237] [1855]. Each policy was set aside by the District of Massachusetts, in rulings that relied on the rule of acceptance, and none was in effect on October 4, 2026 [24] [237] [289] [1855] [1854] [1853]. The two share a number and little else, since the de minimis rate is an option for an organisation with no negotiated rate and the caps were limits placed on rates already negotiated [1802] [24]. Executive Order 14332 directs OMB to revise the Uniform Guidance to limit facilities and administrative costs; no such revision has taken effect [246] [1480].

<!-- figure: indirect-cost-rules -->

Cost sharing, also called matching, is the part of a project's cost not paid by federal funds, and the funding notice must state whether it is required [1880] [1848]. For research grants the Uniform Guidance says voluntary committed cost sharing is not expected [1814].

Termination ends an award before the planned end of the period of performance [1799]. 2 CFR 200.340 lists four ways it can happen: the recipient's failure to comply, the recipient's consent, the recipient's own written notice, and the terms of the award itself [1801]. The fourth includes, to the extent authorised by law, an award that "no longer effectuates the program goals or agency priorities", and every termination provision has to be stated clearly and unambiguously in the award [1801]. The rule in force has no ground called termination for convenience [1801]. Executive Order 14332 directs that discretionary grants permit one, through a revision of the Uniform Guidance, which is the frozen proposal, and through new terms that depend on each agency [246] [1480] [65].

On July 17, 2026 the District of Massachusetts, in New Jersey v. OMB, declared that the fourth ground does not reach priorities adopted after the award was made [1869]. The court granted no vacatur or injunction, and whether the judgment was appealed was not found [1869] [722]. Twenty States, three governors and the District of Columbia had sought the declaration, and a court order protects only the parties and classes it names [1869] [1867]. OMB's proposed text would add a ground for termination in the agency's interest, measured against priorities "as they exist at the time of termination", which is the reading the July judgment declared the present clause does not carry [2104] [1869]. <!-- synthesis -->

A recipient contesting a termination has the agency's own procedures and a suit in federal court [1828] [28]. At HHS the Departmental Appeals Board hears a termination for failure to comply with an award's terms, yet ruled on December 30, 2025 that it has no jurisdiction over an NIH termination based on non-alignment with agency priorities [1888] [132]. NSF states that priority-based terminations are the final agency decision and not appealable to NSF, while its PAPPG gives 30 days to ask for review and its 2026 General Conditions include a termination review procedure [154] [261] [263]. In court the question since 2025 has been which court: the Supreme Court has twice indicated that a claim to be paid under a terminated grant belongs in the Court of Federal Claims, while a challenge to an agency-wide policy may be heard in a district court [1804] [28]. No Supreme Court merits decision on the forum question was found [1867]. The meaning of the rule is thus argued in one court and the money in another [1869] [1804]. <!-- synthesis -->

## The research funders

A newcomer to research funding tends to picture a call: an agency names a topic and a date, and proposals answer it. The ordinary NIH route runs the other way, through the parent announcement, a standing, broad notice for an application whose topic the investigator chose and NIH did not ask for [1]. What is fixed is the calendar, three cycles a year, with new R01 applications due on February 5, June 5 and October 5 [1] [2] [121]. An institute's wishes are signalled separately, through Highlighted Topics, which are not funding opportunities and carry no guaranteed funds; the application still goes in through a parent announcement [11] [142] [94].

Since September 25, 2025 NIH accepts six applications per calendar year from one PD/PI, the programme director or principal investigator, across all activity codes except T codes and the R13 [12] [140]. A notice of June 8, 2026 asked for comment on a proposed cap of 2, 3 or 4 research project grants per person, and NIH announced no decision on August 19, 2026 [89] [88]. NIH reported that the success rate for research project grants fell from 18.5% in FY 2024 to 13.0% in FY 2025 [81]. Each investigator may now use the standing notice less often, and a smaller share of research project applications was funded in FY 2025 than the year before [12] [81]. <!-- synthesis -->

Review at NIH keeps two levels: a scientific review group, called a study section, then the institute's advisory council, which makes recommendations to the director, who decides [38] [15]. Since 2025 the Center for Scientific Review organizes the first level for all NIH applications, where 23 institute review branches had handled about 22 percent of them [55] [54] [40]. For due dates from January 25, 2025, reviewers of most research project applications score two factors from 1 to 9 and rate a third as sufficient or insufficient [39] [41]. Executive Order 14332 of August 7, 2025 placed a senior appointee's review of new announcements and discretionary grants on top of merit review [246].

NSF has no single calendar; each opportunity sets its own timing [156]. The ordinary route is a full proposal to a programme description, programme announcement or solicitation, which NSF may fund as a grant after merit review [156] [157]. Several directorates gather core research into a few solicitations, BIO Core for biology and GEO Core for geoscience among them, and these take proposals at any time [185] [205] [206]. Where a date appears, its kind matters: after a target date a proposal is still accepted but may miss a panel, and after a deadline date it is not accepted [156].

Some NSF proposals begin as something shorter. For RAPID, the type for time-sensitive work, and EAGER, for exploratory work on untested ideas, the investigator first sends the programme officer a concept outline, generally limited to two pages or 6000 characters, so that the officer can judge fit [156] [251] [257] [250].

Merit review at NSF means two criteria, Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts, applied by reviewers whom a programme officer gathers before recommending award or decline [157] [160]. Since December 8, 2025 a full proposal needs at least two reviewers, who may be NSF staff [149]. NSF also returns unreviewed proposals it judges not aligned with agency priorities [154]. The organisation behind the review has been redrawn: MPS turned five divisions into five sections and EDU four divisions into two clusters [177] [178] [181]. NSF's printed proposal guide places final programmatic approval at division or office level, a level these directorates have since renamed or removed [157] [177] [181]. <!-- synthesis -->

The postings changed too. On August 17, 2026 NSF released twelve Notices of Funding Opportunity for foundational research in BIO, CISE, ENG, GEO and MPS, which consolidate earlier publications; none is for SBE, EDU or TIP [192] [185] [197] [199]. The programme pages of SBE, the social, behavioral and economic sciences directorate, updated in July and September 2026, show no upcoming due dates and state that each programme is waiting for a new publication [224] [226] [225]. NSF's FY 2027 budget request of April 3, 2026, a proposal, asks $0 for SBE and says NSF will close out that directorate in FY 2027 [255].

Defense, Energy and NASA, the mission agencies, work from standing notices too, and at the Defense Department that notice is the broad agency announcement, or BAA, which invites research proposals across an office's fields of interest [334] [277] [371]. The announcement names the programme officer for each research area, and the offices encourage contact before a proposal [339] [334] [341]. A white paper usually comes next, a short description the officer can encourage or discourage; ONR limits it to 5 pages [334] [336]. The Energy Department's Office of Science keeps one annual notice open for twelve months and takes a pre-application where a review panel requires one [277] [364] [278].

NASA's ROSES, Research Opportunities in Space and Earth Science, is a single omnibus announcement holding many programme elements, each with its own scope and due dates [371]. Where an element requires a notice of intent or a Step-1 proposal, a full proposal without one on time is returned without review [377] [374]. A standing notice can also stop: AFOSR's open announcement closed on March 23, 2026 with a notice citing Executive Order 14332, and its successor was posted on June 4, 2026 [319] [320]. Across the three agencies the full proposal is a late step, reached after a named officer and a shorter paper [334] [277] [377]. <!-- synthesis -->

Among the smaller funders, AHRQ, the health services research agency, kept its R01, R03 and R18 as standing announcements until, between March 6 and March 17, 2026, it ended them by notices of early expiration that give no reason and no reissue date [507] [508] [509] [512] [515] [516]. The NIOSH standing R01 and R21 announcements expired on November 21, 2025; forecasts for their successors were posted on Grants.gov in July 2026, with estimated posting dates of November 12 and December 16, 2026 [556] [558] [557] [2534] [2535]. EPA's research funding page, last updated April 29, 2026, states there are no open funding opportunities, and the last STAR research notice on Grants.gov was posted July 31, 2024 [452] [461].

Elsewhere competitions run on terms set notice by notice: IES, the education research institute, announced on August 6, 2026 that FY 2027 grantmaking runs in three tranches, with Education Research Grants planned for December 2026 [661] [662]. NIJ states that most of its justice research notices are typically released December through May, subject to appropriations [601] [610] [607]. Two FY 2026 notices from NIFA, the agricultural science funder, tell applicants that projects are to align with a USDA memorandum of December 30, 2025 naming five priorities [418] [412] [414]. NEH's 2026 competitions for Collaborative Research and for Scholarly Editions and Translations took only projects on American history and culture or Western civilization [633] [632]. For these funders a programme's description and its current notice are separate facts, and the description often outlasts the notice [512] [556] [461]. <!-- synthesis -->

## Money that follows a person

A newcomer hears the word fellowship and pictures a person filling in a form and later being paid. Sometimes that is the arrangement. In NSF's Graduate Research Fellowship and the defence department's NDSEG fellowship the student is the applicant [818] [913]. In the NIH F31 and NASA's FINESST the university applies with the student named [784] [949]. A third arrangement takes the student out of the application altogether: under GAANN and NSF's EPSCoR Graduate Fellowship Program a department or university wins the award and then chooses its fellows [1016] [825].

Nothing in an award's name says which arrangement applies. Inside NSF alone, the biology postdoctoral fellowship is applied for by the person and awarded to the individual, while the graduate fellowship is applied for by the person and its funds are awarded to the institution where the fellow enrols [837] [818]. The NIH postdoctoral fellowship, the F32, is submitted by the applicant organisation [784] [737]. The split therefore runs through each agency and not between agencies, so knowing the funder does not settle who applies [837] [818] [784]. <!-- synthesis -->

The amount is not in the programme's description either. NIH sets the stipends for its NRSA awards, the line that covers its fellowships and training grants, once a fiscal year in a Guide notice, after its appropriation [732]. A notice of March 18, 2026 set the FY 2026 predoctoral stipend at $29,364 for awards made on or after October 1, 2025; the FY 2025 level was $28,788 [732] [733]. Postdoctoral stipends for FY 2026 run from $63,480 at 0 years of experience to $77,076 at 7 or more [732]. The levels apply only to awards made with that year's funds, and earlier awards are not adjusted [732].

<!-- figure: stipend-levels -->

Citizenship rules sit in the same notices. An NIH fellow must be a citizen, a non-citizen national or a permanent resident by the time of award, and a trainee on an NIH training grant by the time of appointment [736] [750]. NDSEG does not admit permanent residents, and the NIST associateships require citizenship [914] [912] [978]. The Hubble and NOAA climate fellowships do not require it [946] [973].

Some of this money carries a debt of service. A person incurs a service payback obligation for the first 12 months of postdoctoral NRSA support [737]. It is discharged by an equal number of months of further postdoctoral NRSA support, or of health-related research, research training or teaching averaging at least 20 hours a week [769]. NIH may extend the time, waive the obligation or require repayment with interest within 3 years [769]. Loan repayment states its bargain at the outset: NIH repays up to $50,000 a year of educational debt for health professionals who do qualified research for at least 20 hours a week for at least two years, and an NIH grant is not needed to qualify [762] [763]. The obligation binds the person even where, as with the F32, an organisation submitted the application [737] [784] [769]. <!-- synthesis -->

Further along a career the applicant is the institution, which submits a mentored career development application for a named candidate and an early-career faculty proposal on behalf of one investigator [744] [815] [924]. The candidate for one of NIH's mentored K awards names a mentor and commits at least 75 percent of full-time professional effort; the parent notices state no single NIH-wide salary figure, since each participating institute sets its own level [744]. An earlier success closes the door: current and former principal investigators of an NIH R01 are not eligible for a mentored K award [744]. The K99/R00, for a postdoctoral researcher moving to a faculty post, admits no more than 4 years of postdoctoral research experience on the application due date and has no citizenship requirement [743].

Each early-career faculty programme measures "early" its own way [815] [890]. NSF's CAREER requires an untenured investigator and allows three competitions; the Energy Department counts ten years from the doctorate and the Army five [815] [890] [341]. The test is set in each year's notice, and the Air Force's FY 2027 announcement required a doctorate received on or after April 1, 2019 [924]. Tenure status and years since the doctorate are different clocks, so one researcher can stand inside one programme's window and outside another's on the same day [815] [890] [341]. <!-- synthesis -->

The institutional training grant is the plainest case of an award a person never applies for. In the NIH T32 an institution applies through a programme director and appoints trainees, who receive NIH stipends and do not apply to the funder [750] [732]. NIH reimburses indirect costs on training grants and K awards at 8% of modified total direct costs, not at the organisation's negotiated rate, and on training grants the base excludes tuition and fees [744] [750]. An individual fellowship pays none; it carries an institutional allowance, $4,750 at the predoctoral level for FY 2026 [736] [732]. GAANN requires the grantee to add at least 25 percent of the grant amount from non-Federal funds, and in NSF's graduate fellowship the institution bears any tuition and fees above the $16,000 cost-of-education allowance [1017] [818]. Money that follows a person can thus leave a cost with the institution that hosts the person [750] [1017] [818]. <!-- synthesis -->

Notices also end, and in 2025 and 2026 several did. Grants.gov shows NIH's F31 diversity notice archived with a close date of February 21, 2025, and NIH reissued its five parent fellowship notices on June 13, 2025 [736] [737] [739] [738] [784] [800]. The Education Department cancelled the fiscal year 2025 Fulbright-Hays Doctoral Dissertation Research Abroad competition on May 9, 2025 [1011]. AHRQ declared its dissertation grant notice expired on March 6, 2026, and its K08 and K18 notices expired effective immediately on March 17 and March 20, 2026 [513] [782] [812] [770] [783] [771] [772]. ## Funding a programme or a service

A non-profit that runs a shelter or a treatment service finds a federal programme that pays for exactly that work and assumes that the next step is an application to the agency named on it. Often no such step exists. On the formula route the money goes by formula or block grant to a state, territory, tribe or local government, and that body, not the federal agency, funds local organisations [1280] [1241] [1292]. HUD says it does not provide Community Development Block Grant assistance directly to non-profits, and FEMA's notice for the Nonprofit Security Grant Program says non-profit organisations may not apply to FEMA directly [1292] [1320].

A formula grant is an allocation to states, territories or local governments by a distribution formula in the law or regulations [1880]. Several of the largest health programmes are made this way, among them the two SAMHSA block grants and the Title V Maternal and Child Health block grant; they are awarded to states, territories or cities, which then fund local providers [1129] [1130] [1133] [1201] [1168] [1167] [1158] [1193] [1219]. Older Americans Act money passes from ACL to 56 state agencies on aging, then to 618 area agencies on aging, then to nearly 20,000 local service providers [1260].

What a federal record can do is name the door. For the mental health block grant it is the State Mental Health Authority, and for 21st Century Community Learning Centers the state educational agency [1219] [1399]. The calendar belongs to that body too: each state sets its own competition dates for the learning centres, and a non-profit that wants a FEMA security grant applies to its State Administrative Agency, which sets an earlier deadline of its own [1399] [1320]. Past the door the federal record goes quiet. The pages for Title V and Ryan White Parts A and B do not say how a local organisation applies [1168] [1158] [1156].

<!-- figure: formula-state-door -->

The other route is the direct competition, opened by a notice of funding opportunity, the formal announcement that federal funding is available [1799]. The Uniform Guidance sets a standard of at least 60 calendar days open and states that no opportunity is to be open for fewer than 30 unless the agency finds exigent circumstances, though it is worded as an expectation, not a requirement [1800]. SAMHSA's FY 2026 notices were posted between June 15 and July 1, 2026, and most closed in about a month; two ran from July 1 to July 27 [1109] [1208] [1207]. The Basic Center notice for runaway and homeless youth stayed open about two months a year until 2024, then 19 days in 2025 and 25 days in 2026 [1233]. Recent Labor Department windows ran seven to ten weeks [1297] [1299].

One SAMHSA notice says to register in the agency's system at least six weeks before the deadline [1112] [1204]. Six weeks is longer than a window of July 1 to July 27, and the Basic Center's 19 and 25 days fall under the floor the rule describes [1112] [1208] [1207] [1233] [1800]. <!-- synthesis --> Nor does every programme open each year. The latest New Access Points notice closed August 30, 2024, and a Head Start agency is designated for a service area for five years and renewed without competition unless it meets one of seven conditions [1153] [1170] [1226].

SAMHSA lists its notices on a Grants Dashboard and posts them on Grants.gov [549] [1107] [1113] [1112] [548]. An application that is late, over the page limit or missing a required attachment is screened out and not reviewed [549] [1107] [1113] [1112] [548]. Peer reviewers score the project narrative against point-weighted sections in the notice; the programme office and approving official make the final determination, and awards over $250,000 need National Advisory Council approval [549] [1107] [1113] [1112] [548]. HRSA takes some applications in two phases, the first in Grants.gov and the second, by a later deadline, in its Electronic Handbooks [1194] [1150]. There a merit review panel of independent experts scores the applications that pass screening, and HRSA may then apply funding factors and a risk review before deciding [1145] [1144] [1147] [1194] [1215] [1204] [1151]. Even a direct door can pass a state office. The FY 2026 notice for Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinics required a letter from the state, district or territory behavioural health authority approving the application [1112] [548].

Cost sharing, also called matching, is the part of project cost not paid by federal funds, and a notice must state whether it is required and say so when it is not [1880] [1848]. The requirement varies by programme, even inside one agency [1208] [1206] [1249] [1346]. <!-- synthesis --> Most SAMHSA notices here require none, yet Building Communities of Recovery caps the federal share at 85 percent [1208] [1206]. At ACF, Head Start requires 20 percent of programme cost and the culturally specific family violence grant nothing [1249] [1346]. The bases differ as well: 25 percent of the federal share for YouthBuild, at least 50 percent of total project cost for GEAR UP [1356] [1402].

An Education Department competition may now be run by another department [672] [1382]. Under interagency agreements announced November 18, 2025 and February 23, 2026, the Department of Labor, HHS, Interior and State run competitions for named Education programmes, which keep their Education Assistance Listing numbers and statutes [672] [1397] [1398] [1382]. The November agreements are recorded as pending implementation, and Labor's Employment and Training Administration ran FY 2026 competitions noticed from March 23, 2026 [672] [1382] [1384] [1396] [1395].

The conditions that come with an award have in several cases been fixed by a court [1479] [1276]. An HHS notice of July 14, 2025 added 13 programmes, among them the Health Center Program and Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinics, to what the department reads as a "federal public benefit" under the statute PRWORA [1175] [1177]. On September 21, 2026 the District of Rhode Island vacated that notice and permanently enjoined its enforcement against the plaintiff states and the District of Columbia [1175] [1177] [1178] [1479]. FY2026 notices of the Justice Department's Office of Justice Programs list as unallowable any activity that violates federal immigration law or federal civil rights law, and in one DOJ states it has stipulated not to enforce those paragraphs while the amended preliminary injunction of July 2, 2026 in Freedom Network v. Trump remains in effect [1274] [1276].

Some orders reach only those who sued [544] [1389]. A preliminary injunction of May 16, 2025 against HHS's termination, on March 24 and 25, 2025, of about $11 billion in CDC and SAMHSA grants covers 23 plaintiff states, the District of Columbia and their local health jurisdictions [544] [1210]. When the District of Columbia district court on January 16, 2026 vacated the Education Department's 2025 decisions to deny new Student Support Services applications and discontinue existing TRIO grants, it did so for the plaintiff association's named members [1389]. Two non-profits holding the same kind of award can thus stand under different terms, according to the state in which they work or the association they joined [544] [1389]. <!-- synthesis -->

## Foundations

A newcomer tends to picture a foundation as an endowment with an application form, and to assume that a bigger endowment means a wider door. The records behind this section say otherwise. They cover 65 foundations and other private grantmakers, a sample chosen by the editor, each stating on its own pages how it takes requests [2124] [2290]. Counted from those records, six accept an unsolicited request [2310] [2426] [2418]. Twenty-three fund by invitation only, twenty-three take applications only under an open call they have posted, ten begin with a letter of inquiry, and for three no answer is recorded [2211] [2119] [2216] [2448].

The word covers several legal kinds. Under the tax code a section 501(c)(3) organisation is a private foundation unless it falls into an excluded category, the public charities [2237] [2238]. A private foundation files Form 990-PF each year and pays an excise tax on net investment income [2237]. A community foundation that meets a public support test is a public charity and reports on Form 990 [2374]. A company may give through a company-sponsored foundation, itself a private foundation, or through a giving programme inside the company that files no public return [2408] [2387]. Some grantmakers in the sample, The Pew Charitable Trusts among them, file Form 990 instead [2176].

The invitation-only foundations say so in plain words. Ford: "We do not accept any unsolicited grant proposals by mail, email or phone." [2259] Mellon's staff "seek out and get to know innovative organizations, scholars, and creators before inviting them to submit a proposal" [2210]. Mott has not accepted unsolicited letters of inquiry or proposals since January 2025 [2298]. At Packard less than one percent of grants come from unsolicited proposals [2283].

<!-- figure: foundation-doors -->

A letter of inquiry is a wider door, though a conditional one. The foundation reads a short first document and invites a full proposal from some applicants; one sent without an invitation is not read [2124] [2216] [2154] [2219]. The Doris Duke Foundation answers within two months and adds, "Very few grants result from unsolicited letters of inquiry" [2178]. The six that accept requests are all local or in-kind: two community foundations with a home region, the Duke Endowment in North Carolina and South Carolina, Woodruff in Georgia, and two technology companies' programmes that give products [2418] [2425] [2309] [2313] [2446] [2447]. <!-- synthesis -->

Kind shapes the door too. A community foundation pays out in two ways and only one takes applications: competitive grants are decided by its staff and board, while donor-advised grants follow the advice of the donor who set up the fund [2416] [2428] [2373]. The New York Community Trust tells applicants, "We cannot refer you to donors" [2416]. The corporate funders read keep an open route for small local grants or a fixed annual window and make national grants by invitation [2433] [2441] [2438].

Size is a poor guide to any of it. Lilly Endowment, whose return for the year ending December 31, 2025 shows total assets of $105,894,116,636, starts with a preliminary letter of no more than two pages, sent by mail [2279] [2277]. The Gates Foundation, with a trust endowment of $92 billion as of June 30, 2026, unaudited, has no general application [2254] [2250]. The two largest asset figures in these records thus stand behind opposite doors. <!-- synthesis -->

Awards for people add a filter that the applicant's own employer applies. Many foundation fellowships and scholar awards cannot be applied for directly: the institution nominates under a cap the funder sets, and so chooses among its own faculty before the funder sees anything [2174] [2175]. Dreyfus takes one nomination per institution a year for each Teacher-Scholar programme [2146] [2147]. Some lists are closed altogether. Pew took one nominee from each of 212 participating institutions for its 2027 award and Packard up to two from each of 50 invited institutions in 2026; none of the pages read gives a way for an institution to ask to be added [2174] [2149] [2212]. The Cottrell Scholar Award has neither cap nor nomination [2141].

Indirect costs differ just as widely, in kind as well as amount. A foundation does not pay a university's federally negotiated rate, which section 3 describes; each sets its own limit, and there is no common rule [2120] [2133] [2129] [2156]. Keck pays nothing and treats the institution's waiver as in-kind support [2133] [2135]. Pew allows not more than 8 percent ($24,000) of the total award, and Simons 20 percent of direct costs with some items left out of the base [2174] [2120]. Then the direction reverses: Ford sets a minimum of 25 percent on project grants from January 1, 2023, and MacArthur adds a flat 29 percent to project costs [2332] [2265].

<!-- figure: foundation-indirect-rates -->

The law does set a floor under spending. A private non-operating foundation must pay out a minimum amount each year, reckoned from 5 percent of the value of its assets not used for exempt purposes [2375] [2377]. MacArthur raised its own baseline to 6 percent in February 2025 and has announced that the 6 percent runs through 2028, after which it intends a target of 5.25 percent [2326] [2328]. Gates announced on May 8, 2025 that it will spend more than $200 billion over twenty years and close in 2045, the one end date in these records [2252]. Whether a foundation takes requests is a separate matter, and its tax return shows that too [2375] [2387].

Form 990-PF is public: a foundation must make it available for inspection and copying for three years [2461]. Part XIV of the 2025 form lists every grant, and its line 2 opens with a check box for a foundation that "only makes contributions to preselected charitable organizations and does not accept unsolicited requests" for funds [2387]. A foundation that leaves the box unchecked fills in lines 2a to 2d with a contact, a form of application, deadlines and restrictions [2387]. In two e-filed returns for tax year 2024 the box was marked in the Kavli Foundation's and absent from the Dreyfus Foundation's [2481] [2480]. Both agree with the funders' own pages, where Kavli gives no route but a posted call and Dreyfus takes nominations [2138] [2146]. <!-- synthesis -->

What the filing covers is narrower than the field. The line exists only for private foundations that had $5,000 or more in assets during the year, and a community foundation, as a public charity filing Form 990, has no such line [2387] [2374]. The box is the foundation's own statement for the tax year of the return; its website may say something newer [2387]. An answer can be a bare pointer: the Dreyfus return refers to its website for both deadlines and restrictions [2480]. The IRS bulk files hold only electronically filed returns, with 2019 through 2026 listed on 2026-10-04, and its search page says paper-filed returns received 2021 and later are still being processed [2397] [2059].

## Standing and capacity

A newcomer to federal funding assumes that eligibility is a question about the project: whether the idea is strong and the people can carry it out. For a large family of programmes the question is settled earlier, and somewhere else. NIH's Institutional Development Award programme (IDeA) settles it by location, since an institution qualifies only if it is in a state on NIH's list [1546]. The Education Department's formula grants for historically Black colleges go to a class that is closed, fixed by statute and by a list in regulation [1602] [1649].

Geography comes first. NSF's EPSCoR is a programme to raise the research competitiveness of targeted jurisdictions, and IDeA, its NIH counterpart, was authorised by Congress in 1993 for states with historically low NIH grant funding success rates [866] [1545]. Energy, NASA, Defense and USDA run counterparts, and no single list serves them all [1567] [1570] [1565] [1576]. NSF names 28 jurisdictions, on a list frozen through FY 2027, and the Energy Department and NASA follow it [1517] [1567] [1574]. Defense publishes its own in each year's notice, and the FY26 notices posted August 10, 2026 carry 36 entries [1565]. USDA lists 19 states for FY 2026, plus the District of Columbia and insular areas [1576]. NIH lists 23 states and Puerto Rico [1543].

Alabama and Iowa are on the NSF, Energy, NASA and Defense lists and on neither the NIH list nor USDA's for FY 2026 [1517] [1567] [1573] [1565] [1543] [1576]. Nor are the rules behind the lists equally visible. USDA states its test: funding no higher than the 38th percentile of all states on a three-year rolling average of AFRI funding levels [1576]. The NIH pages and notices read do not state the rule by which a state enters or leaves the IDeA list, and the Defense listings do not state theirs [1543] [1545] [1565]. An "EPSCoR state" is therefore not one thing, and a research office establishes its standing one agency at a time [1517] [1565] [1576] [1543]. <!-- synthesis -->

<!-- figure: jurisdiction-lists -->

Standing of this kind also brings money that needs no separate application [1518]. NSF's EPSCoR often co-funds proposals sent to other NSF programmes when the applicant is in an EPSCoR jurisdiction [1518]. NIGMS adds up to $350,000 in total costs for each of the first two years to R01, R15 and MIRA R35 applications from IDeA states that an NIH institute did not select for funding [1553].

A second kind of standing attaches to what an institution is, and what matters is how the designation is held. Historically Black colleges and universities are listed by state in 34 CFR 608.2, and current enrolment plays no part [1649]. Tribal colleges are likewise defined by statute [1617]. Hispanic-serving status works the other way. It requires undergraduate full-time-equivalent enrolment that is at least 25 percent Hispanic at the end of the award year before the institution applies, and it is shown at application, not kept as a permanent list [1647] [1642].

That difference mattered in 2025 and 2026. On September 10, 2025 the Education Department announced that it would no longer award discretionary grants under seven programmes, and on September 18, 2025 it withdrew the FY 2025 notices for Developing Hispanic-Serving Institutions and three other programmes and cancelled the competitions [1594] [1643]. An Office of Legal Counsel opinion of December 2, 2025 concluded that the eligibility provisions of seven Education programmes are unconstitutional and inseverable [1592]. A second, of August 12, 2026, reached that conclusion for NSF's Hispanic-serving institutions programme and concluded that NSF awards to tribal colleges are constitutional; neither opinion addresses HBCU programmes [876] [1592]. Each institutional class the opinions reach in these records is defined by a share of enrolment, while the classes fixed by statute were upheld or left unmentioned [1592] [876] [1647] [1649]. <!-- synthesis --> A lawsuit of June 11, 2025 over the Developing HSIs programme had reached a summary judgment motion, filed August 31, 2026, with no ruling on the intervenor's motions reported [1607] [1650]. Section 10 gives the dated account.

Instrumentation and facilities grants are defined by their object: they pay for an instrument or research space, not for a research project [1534] [1541]. NIH's S10 Shared Instrumentation Grant buys one commercially available instrument, at $300,000 to $5,000,000, for a group with at least three Major Users holding active NIH research awards [1534] [1536]. NSF's Major Research Instrumentation programme (MRI) pays for a single shared instrument of more than $100,000 and up to $4,000,000 [1513] [1516]. NSF announced on July 2, 2025 that it would not accept new MRI proposals in the FY 2026 window, October 15 to November 14, 2025, and anticipates an updated solicitation for proposals in FY 2027 [1514] [1513].

Many capacity programmes also cap what one institution may send [1516] [1537] [1542]. Under the last MRI solicitation the limit was two proposals in Track 1 and one each in Tracks 2 and 3 [1516]. NIH's C06 construction awards take one application per institution, and the COBRE Development phase, an IDeA programme, takes one from an institution with no active COBRE grant [1542] [1545]. Where a cap exists the first competition is internal, and it is over before the funder sees anything [1516] [1542] [1545]. <!-- synthesis -->

The last route leaves agency competitions altogether. Congress may name a specific recipient, project and amount in an appropriations act; the House calls this Community Project Funding and the Senate Congressionally Directed Spending [1667] [1674]. An organisation cannot apply to either committee [1667] [1674]. It asks its House Member or a Senator, on that office's own deadline, and the office decides whether to submit the request [1667] [1674]. Requests are capped, at 20 per House Member across all bills in FY27, and that year's House guidance excludes non-profits, other than fire stations, from Community Facilities requests [1667] [1672]. Several of the accounts opened to requests pay for buildings: the House FY27 list includes Community Facilities, ARS Buildings and Facilities and HUD Economic Development Initiatives, and the Senate's adds NIST Construction of Research Facilities [1669] [1675]. <!-- synthesis -->

Being named in the act does not release the money [1686] [1672]. The named recipient still applies to the agency, which checks the project against the congressional direction and its own rules [1686] [1672]. Each agency sets its own step, sometimes late: the Labor Department's FY 2026 guidance letter was issued on August 21, 2026, about six and a half months after the act was signed on February 3, 2026 [1686] [1690]. GAO estimated that 61 percent of the $39 billion designated for FY2022 to FY2024 had been obligated and about 16 percent paid out by the end of FY 2024 [1681]. The route does not run every year: Congress designated no such funds in FY 2025, and FY2026 projects were enacted in four laws signed between November 12, 2025 and April 30, 2026 [1680] [1755] [1677]. The standing lasts for that act's project only [1667] [1675].

<!-- section: 9-the-gates -->
## The gates

A newcomer reading a funding notice tends to treat its deadline as the date that counts. The gates stand well before it. An organisation that applies to a federal agency as the prime applicant must first hold an active registration in SAM.gov, the registry run by the General Services Administration [1897] [1899]. Registration assigns the Unique Entity ID, or UEI, which every later registration uses [1898] [784]. Most agencies then take applications through Grants.gov, and some add a system of their own, such as eRA Commons at NIH [1895] [1911]. Registering is free [1898]. The price is paid in weeks, and in upkeep afterwards. <!-- synthesis -->

The steps belong to different people. Each person creates a Login.gov account, which belongs to that person and not to the organisation [1897] [1932]. The organisation registers in SAM.gov with its legal name and physical address, which SAM.gov validates before assigning the UEI [1897] [1898]. The person named there as Electronic Business Point of Contact then registers the organisation on Grants.gov and assigns roles; someone must hold the Authorized Organization Representative role to submit [1895]. At NIH a Signing Official registers the organisation in eRA Commons, and every person named on an application needs a personal account [1911] [1909].

How long this takes depends on which official page is read. SAM.gov says a registration can take up to 10 business days to become active [1897]. Grants.gov adds that it could take weeks or months if information is not available or verified [1895]. NIH says to allow six weeks or more for the whole set of registrations [1909]. NSF advises starting SAM registration at least 90 days before submission [161]. The registry's figure is the shortest of these, and the agencies that depend on the registry plan for longer. <!-- synthesis --> The Department of Justice does not accept late registration as a reason for a late application [619].

<!-- figure: registration-times -->

Nor does a registration stay done. The SAM.gov registration must be renewed every 365 days [1897]. Under 2 CFR 25.200(b)(2) it has to be current and active whenever the entity has an active award or an application under consideration, and under 2 CFR 25.205 an agency may not issue an award, or add funds to one, to an entity out of compliance [1899]. NSF notes that a lapsed registration takes longer to reactivate than one renewed while active [156]. Personal accounts lapse as well: a Grants.gov account is deactivated after 365 days of inactivity [1966].

An organisation that joins someone else's application faces a lower gate. A subrecipient receives a subaward from a pass-through entity, the body that passes federal money on, to carry out part of a federal award [1799] [1805]. Under 2 CFR 25.300 it must have a UEI and is not required to complete full registration [1899] [1900]. An entity holding only a UEI cannot apply directly for federal awards [1897]. The terms are no lighter. Federal terms flow down to the subrecipient, and money received as a subrecipient counts toward the audit threshold described below, while payments to a contractor do not [1805] [1845].

Research money adds duties that attach to the institution. One engaged in non-exempt human subjects research, supported by an agency that follows the Common Rule on such research, needs a Federalwide Assurance, filed with the Office for Human Research Protections and renewed every 5 years even if nothing has changed [1986] [1984] [1988]. An institution without its own review board may rely on another registered one [1981]. Public Health Service research funding requires a written, enforced financial conflict of interest policy, and an assurance on research misconduct kept active by an annual report filed between January 1 and April 30 [2002] [2009] [2007]. Research security training has to be certified as completed within the 12 months before submission, at NSF from December 2, 2025 and at NIH for due dates on or after May 25, 2026 [2014] [33].

One assurance cannot be had in advance. No activity with live vertebrate animals that the Public Health Service supports may be conducted until the institution has an Animal Welfare Assurance negotiated with NIH's Office of Laboratory Animal Welfare, and that office does not accept unsolicited applications [1994]. The funding component first notifies the institution and the office of a pending award; the office then makes contact, and materials sent earlier are not processed [1994]. Its pages give no time for the negotiation [1994]. Among the assurances described here, it is the only one an institution cannot begin on its own initiative. <!-- synthesis -->

For a non-profit there is a gate before all of these. An organisation becomes a non-profit under state law and tax-exempt under federal law, in two separate steps [2047]. Recognition under section 501(c)(3) comes from the IRS on Form 1023, with a user fee of $600, or, if the organisation is small enough, on the shorter Form 1023-EZ, with a fee of $275 [2046] [2051]. The IRS says it issues 80% of Form 1023-EZ determinations within 22 days and 80% of Form 1023 determinations within 191 days, on a page last updated June 28, 2026 [2053]. Grants.gov lists two non-profit applicant categories, those with 501(c)(3) status and those without, and each notice states which it admits [2064]. The Arts Endowment and a 2026 NEH notice require the status by name [2065] [648].

The status can be lost by silence. An exempt organisation files a return or an electronic notice every year, and three consecutive years without a filing end the exemption automatically [2055] [2057]. A revoked organisation must apply again, with the user fee [2057] [2058]. A fiscal sponsor, which oversees the fiscal activities of another organisation, did not open a federal door on the two pages read [2065] [648]. The Arts Endowment bars an organisation without its own non-profit status from applying through one, though it may take part in a project submitted by an eligible organisation [2065]. NEH's notice of March 12, 2026 makes fiscal sponsors applying on behalf of an ineligible organisation ineligible [648]. Other agencies' rules were not checked [2047].

The last gate stands after the money. A non-federal entity that expends $1,000,000 or more in federal awards in a fiscal year must have a single or programme-specific audit; the Federal Audit Clearinghouse applies $750,000 to fiscal years that began before October 1, 2024 [1940] [1938]. Below the threshold an entity is exempt from federal audit requirements for that year, though its records must remain available for review [1940]. Audit reports and findings are among the factors an agency may weigh in its risk review before an award [1809]. The gate at the end of one award is therefore part of the gate at the start of the next. <!-- synthesis -->

## What changed in 2025–26, and what is pending

A newcomer would naturally read an agency's announcement as the rule. For 2025 and 2026 that reading often fails: an announced policy may since have been vacated, confined to the parties who sued, or never made final. <!-- synthesis --> A federal court vacated NSF's 15 percent indirect cost policy, NSF states, yet awards issued on or after May 5, 2025 still carry a term applying the policy if NSF is later permitted to apply it [154].

<!-- figure: changes-timeline -->

The caps on indirect cost rates came first. NIH announced a standard 15% rate in place of negotiated rates on February 7, 2025; a district court enjoined the guidance on March 5, 2025 and later vacated it, and the First Circuit affirmed on January 5, 2026 [25] [24]. DOE's cap of April 11, 2025 was vacated on June 30, 2025 by DOE's own account, NSF's on June 20, 2025, and the Defense Department's, set by memoranda of May 14 and June 12, 2025, on October 10, 2025 [288] [238] [293]. NSF gives a second date, May 19, 2025, for its return to negotiated rates [154]. Section 224 of the FY 2026 appropriations act keeps the indirect cost provisions of 45 CFR part 75 applying to NIH grants [62]. The FY 2027 budget request proposes a 15 percent cap on each NIH award [57].

Terminations came in the same months. HHS issued notices on March 24 and 25, 2025 terminating about $11 billion in CDC and SAMHSA public health grants [544]. NEH terminated more than 1,400 grants between April 1 and April 3, 2025, and AmeriCorps 1,031 on April 25, 2025 [642] [1380]. DOT's list marks grants terminated on May 2, 2025 on the stated ground that each "no longer effectuates the program goals or agency priorities"; OJP's notices for 376 awards in April 2025 gave the same ground and cited 2 C.F.R. 200.340(a)(4) [496] [495] [621] [1281].

The litigation turned on which court hears which claim. On April 4, 2025, in Department of Education v. California, the Supreme Court stayed an order requiring continued payment of terminated grants, stating that the Government was likely to show the district court lacked jurisdiction to order payment of money under the APA and pointing to the Court of Federal Claims under the Tucker Act [1804]. Its order of August 21, 2025 stayed judgments that had vacated NIH grant terminations and left in place the vacatur of agency guidance [28]. On May 26, 2026 the Ninth Circuit reversed one injunction on Tucker Act grounds for researchers terminated by form letter and affirmed it for those terminated under the DEI executive orders; the government sought Supreme Court review in August 2026 [240] [242] [243].

## How to read this material, and how the guide was built

The first thing to check on any programme in this guide is its status and the date beside it, because status ages faster than anything else here. The federal research programmes were read a second time on October 5, 2026, one day after the first reading, and sixteen of them showed something the first had not. Grants.gov, for one, carried forecasts for new NIOSH research notices where the earlier reading had found only expired announcements [556] [2534] [2535]. A forecast for a HUD technical-assistance programme went from 45 expected awards to 60 when its listing was updated on October 2, 2026 [1715].

Some things on an official page deserve less trust than they appear to. A Grants.gov listing prints each figure beside a label, and on two SAMHSA listings the figure a reader would take for a ceiling sits under Award Minimum, with no maximum given [1688] [1202]. Agency pages can lag the rule they describe: the regulation sets the de minimis indirect cost rate at up to 15 percent, and NIH's policy text as read still gave 10 percent [1802] [64]. Official estimates of the same task differ, as they do for the time a SAM.gov registration takes [1897] [161]. Where two sources disagree, the guide gives both and leaves the disagreement in view.

For the events of 2025 and 2026 a few distinctions do most of the work. A proposal is not a rule: OMB's rewrite of the grant rules is a proposal, and it cannot be made final before December 11, 2026 [1458] [1480]. A budget request is not an appropriation, which matters when a request asks $0 for a whole directorate [255]. A court order reaches the parties it names, so the same condition can bind one recipient and leave another free of it [544] [1389].

The guide was built from the funders' own pages. Six sweeps read 2,512 of them on October 4 and 5, 2026, through a tool that fetches a page and summarises it, and a later pass over programme status added 51 more. Every source from the sweeps was then fetched a second time and its stored quotations and facts were put to the page. Of 2,512 pages, 2,500 loaded. Of the quotations, 96.7 percent were found word for word and the rest were removed; 93.1 percent of the facts were confirmed in full, and fourteen were corrected after a reading by hand. That second reading used the same tool as the first, and for more than two pages in five it returned only part of the page, so it is a second opinion and not a proof. A source with a fact the second reading did not find stays flagged in the references.
