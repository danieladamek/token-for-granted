## What it is

A newcomer tends to assume that a panel of experts reads an application and picks the winners. In federal grant-making the experts advise and an official of the agency decides. NIH has two levels of review [38]. The first is a scientific review group, also called a study section; the Center for Scientific Review organises these groups and clusters them into review branches by discipline [38] [40]. The second is the national advisory council or board of the institute that would pay, which makes funding recommendations to the institute or center director, and the director decides [38]. For most research project applications with due dates from January 25, 2025, a study section works under a simplified framework: Importance of the Research and Rigor and Feasibility are each scored from 1 to 9, and Expertise and Resources is rated sufficient or insufficient [39] [41].

The outcome of review comes back in a summary statement, which an AHRQ page last reviewed in October 2014 calls a statement "with the review results" [506]. Under practices NIH modified on November 24, 2025 and has continued through the January 2027 council round, 30-35% of applications are discussed in most meetings, the committee's middle third is designated 'competitive but not discussed', and summary statements carry no narrative paragraphs summarising the committee's discussion [126] [16] [84]. A payline was a cut-off in overall impact scores that settled which applications an institute would fund, and about half of NIH's institutes, centers and offices had used one [123] [13]. Effective with the January 2026 council round they "will not rely on funding paylines in developing pay plans" [13]. Each funding policy follows six tenets, among them scientific merit and the availability of funds; peer review information is weighed "in its entirety", and each institute publishes the factors it uses [123] [15].

At NSF, merit review applies two criteria to every proposal, Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts [157] [160]. A programme officer obtains reviews from ad hoc reviewers, a panel or both, and makes a recommendation to award or decline; the policy guide as printed places final programmatic approval at division or office level [157] [160]. After a realignment of December 2025, the pages read do not name the post that now gives it [168] [177]. Since December 8, 2025 a full proposal needs at least two reviewers, who may be NSF staff [149]. A programme agency such as SAMHSA has peer reviewers score the project narrative against sections whose points are fixed in the notice [1113] [1112]. One FY 2026 notice gives 35 points to the implementation approach [1112]. That notice states that "The program office and approving official make the final determination for funding", and that awards over $250,000 need National Advisory Council approval [1112].

A programme officer is the member of agency staff an applicant may talk to. NIH's programme officials can say whether a plan fits their institute and help interpret review feedback; they do not co-write or edit applications or discuss receipt, referral or review details [92] [120]. NSF lists one or more programme officers on each funding opportunity [158]. Each agency also reports the share of applications it funds, by its own measure. NIH gave a success rate of 13.0% for research project grants in FY 2025, 8,161 new and renewal awards from 62,592 applications, against 18.5% in FY 2024 [81]. NSF's budget request shows a funding rate of 19% for competitive awards in its FY 2025 plan, 7,400 awards from 38,700 proposals [255].

## The key idea in one picture

Picture three lanes drawn from left to right, labelled NIH, NSF and SAMHSA, each a row of boxes joined by arrows. The NIH lane reads *study section: scores*, then *advisory council: advises*, then *institute or center director: decides* [38]. The NSF lane reads *reviewers: ad hoc, panel or both*, then *programme officer: recommendation*, then *division or office: approval, as the guide is printed* [157] [160]. The SAMHSA lane reads *peer reviewers: points*, then *programme office and approving official: decide*, with a side box for the National Advisory Council on awards over $250,000 [1112]. Boxes that advise are drawn in outline and boxes that decide are filled, so the filled box is the last in every lane.

Across the top of all three lanes runs one bar, labelled *senior appointee review, Executive Order 14332*. Since that order of August 7, 2025, a senior appointee in each agency reviews new funding opportunity announcements and discretionary grants before they issue, for consistency with agency priorities and the national interest [246]. The bar therefore meets each lane before its first box and again at its last. The order says appointees "shall not ministerially ratify or routinely defer to recommendations of others" [246].

## How this guide uses it

The routes for an NIH research grant, an NSF core proposal and a federal health or behavioural health service grant each carry a review step that rests on this primer, as do the lines on how a funder decides in the funder records. The figure who-decides draws the three lanes, and sections 2 and 4 of the main text give the detail by agency.

Two parts of the NIH lane were unsettled when the guide closed. On August 14, 2026 NIH asked for comment, with responses due October 13, 2026, on a proposal, not in force, to stop reporting final overall impact scores and percentiles and to report three categories instead: 'Most competitive' (top 25%), 'Competitive' (26-50%) and 'Not discussed' (over 50%) [14]. On September 22, 2026 the chair of the Senate Appropriations Committee wrote to the OMB and NIH Directors about an executive order, reported to be in preparation, that would add a review of NIH grant applications after merit review; the release names no order, and the guide's sweep found none [1892].
