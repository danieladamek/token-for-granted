## What it is

A newcomer who has read a federal funding notice tends to treat a foundation as a small agency, with a published programme and a form open to anyone eligible. The law starts somewhere else. Under the tax code every section 501(c)(3) organisation is a private foundation unless it falls into a category the law excludes [2237]. The excluded organisations are the public charities: churches, hospitals, schools, colleges and universities, organisations with many sources of support, and organisations that support them [2238]. A private foundation files Form 990-PF each year and is subject to excise taxes that a public charity is not [2237] [2387]. That return is public and lists every grant paid [2387] [2461].

The names a grant seeker meets sort into a few kinds. The foundations this guide's records label independent or family, Sloan and Moore for example, are private foundations that file Form 990-PF [2126] [2132]. IRS statistics call the grantmaking kind nonoperating; it filed 91.2% of private foundation returns for tax year 2022 [2410]. That kind must pay out a minimum each year, computed from 5 percent of its investment assets [2375] [2377]. An operating foundation devotes most of its resources to its own activities and is not subject to the tax on failure to distribute income [2371]. A corporate foundation is a private foundation whose assets come mainly from a for-profit business; a company may also give through a programme inside the company, which files no public return of its own [2408] [2387]. A community foundation that meets a public support test is a public charity and files Form 990 [2374]. So do some national grantmakers: The Pew Charitable Trusts files Form 990, and HHMI's audited statements for fiscal year 2023 call it a public charity [2176] [2486]. Congress created others to sit beside a federal agency, among them the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health in 1990 and the National Fish and Wildlife Foundation in 1984 [2448] [2452].

Each foundation states on its own pages how it takes requests, and this guide records the answers of 65 foundations and other private grantmakers [2124] [2290]. The 65 are an editorial sample chosen by the editor, not a ranking, and the counts that follow were made from those records. Six accept unsolicited requests, among them The Duke Endowment, the Cleveland Foundation and The New York Community Trust [2310] [2426] [2418]. Ten begin with a letter of inquiry, a short first document after which, in Carnegie Corporation's words, "applicants may be invited via email to submit a full proposal"; Sloan and the Commonwealth Fund work the same way [2216] [2124] [2323]. Twenty-three take applications only under an open call they have posted, as Simons, Spencer and Knight do [2119] [2196] [2296]. Twenty-three fund by invitation only. Ford's page reads "We do not accept any unsolicited grant proposals by mail, email or phone." [2259] Mott has not accepted unsolicited letters of inquiry or proposals since January 2025 [2298]. For three no answer is recorded: the Chicago Community Trust, the Foundation for the NIH and the National Park Foundation [2423] [2448] [2455].

Many foundation fellowships cannot be applied for directly: the institution nominates under a cap the funder sets, and so chooses among its own faculty before the funder sees anything [2174] [2175]. Dreyfus takes one nomination per institution a year for each programme, and Packard took up to two from each of 50 invited institutions in 2026 [2146] [2147] [2149]. Money for organisations comes in two main kinds. General operating support pays for an organisation's work as a whole; project support pays for one named piece of work with its own budget [2261]. Hewlett gives general operating support whenever possible; Woodruff says most of its grants support one-time needs [2280] [2313].

## The key idea in one picture

The first picture is a row of five doors, labelled from left to right in the foundations' terms, each with its count: unsolicited requests accepted, six; letter of inquiry first, ten; open calls only, twenty-three; invitation only, twenty-three; not stated, three [2310] [2216] [2119] [2211] [2448]. Beneath the doors a small check box is drawn, because the tax return asks the same question. Part XIV, line 2 of the 2025 Form 990-PF has a box for a foundation that "only makes contributions to preselected charitable organizations and does not accept unsolicited requests for funds"; one that leaves it unchecked gives a contact, a form of application, deadlines and restrictions on lines 2a to 2d [2387]. The box is the foundation's own statement for the tax year of the return, and its website may say something newer [2387]. A community foundation files Form 990 and has no such line [2374]. Returns can be read free on the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search and on ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer [2059] [2398].

The second picture is a scale of percentages starting at zero, with each foundation's indirect cost figure marked on it. A foundation does not pay a university's federally negotiated rate; each sets its own limit, and there is no common rule [2120] [2133] [2129] [2156]. Keck sits at zero, Moore at 12.5 percent of direct costs, Templeton at 15 percent and Simons at 20 percent of direct costs [2133] [2129] [2156] [2120]. Two marks are drawn differently because they are not ceilings: Ford's 25 percent is a minimum on project grants, and MacArthur adds a flat 29 percent to project costs; neither rate applies to general operating support [2332] [2265]. A note marks a different base: Pew's limit is 8 percent of the total award [2174].

## How this guide uses it

Section 7 of the main text leans on this idea, as do the routes for approaching a foundation, a foundation research grant, a programme grant, a fellowship or scholar award, and general operating support. Each foundation's funder record carries its kind, a field recording how it takes requests, and its indirect cost figure where one was found. The figures foundation-doors and foundation-indirect-rates carry the data behind the two pictures.
